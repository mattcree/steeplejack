# 20 — Building the world

Dimensions for modelling, gathered 2026-09-21. A playtest asked for the visuals to be pushed on and
for reference from the real places, so this is that reference — **as numbers rather than as
pictures**, deliberately. The repo carries no third-party photographs, and a measurement can be
built from where an image can only be looked at.

> **Names.** Rule 16. Nobody is named here. The trade belongs to nobody.

**The main source** for §1 and §2 is an 1885 practical treatise on tall chimney construction, a
survey of eighty-odd existing shafts with dimensioned case studies. Form chronology is from a
borough-level textile mills survey. Where something was not found, it says **not found** — a gap is
more useful than a plausible wrong number, because a plausible wrong number gets built.

## 1. The chimney

### Form follows the material, not the fashion

| Era | Form | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-1850, Pennine | **Square or rectangular**, stone, tapered, short, no oversailer | Local stone, and low engine power needs a small stack |
| Mid-19th c. | **Octagonal**, stone or brick, often on a tall square plinth | "Typified mill-chimney design by the mid-19th century" |
| Later 19th c. on | **Circular**, brick, elaborate oversailers | Brick "could be built taller… and were generally circular" |

Stone cuts to a flat face; brick lays to a curve. That is the whole of the square → octagon → circle
sequence. **The boundary is geological, not the county line**: the Pennine uplands of both Lancashire
and Yorkshire are stone country, the Manchester plain and lowland Lancashire are brick.

A very common hybrid, and a good silhouette: **a square masonry plinth broached to an octagon or a
circle above it.**

### Proportions — build at 1:9 to 1:11

The statutory minima (Metropolitan Board of Works) are base width ≥ **H/10 square, H/11 octagonal,
H/12 circular**. Real mill chimneys are much stouter than the minimum:

| Height | Base across | Ratio | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| 367 ft | ~33.8 ft | 1 : 10.9 | Octagonal brick |
| 300 ft | 30 ft | 1 : 10 | Octagonal stone |
| 300 ft | 37 ft 6 in | 1 : 8 | Circular brick |
| 240 ft | 24 ft | 1 : 10 | Octagonal stone |
| 210 ft | 23 ft 8 in | 1 : 8.9 | Circular brick |
| 150 ft | 16 ft 8 in | 1 : 9 | Circular brick |
| 120 ft | 14 ft | 1 : 8.6 | Circular brick |

Only a deliberately slender campanile-style stack sits at the legal minimum. **1:9 to 1:11 is the
mill chimney.**

### The batter, and the trap in it

The treatise states batter **per face**. The diameter therefore closes at **twice** that rate, and
getting this backwards halves or doubles your taper.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Statutory minimum | 2½ in per 10 ft = **1 : 48 per face** |
| Observed range | 1 : 25 to 1 : 97 |
| **Commonest** | **1 : 40 to 1 : 66 per face** |
| Diameter closure at the minimum | 1 : 24 — **5 in of diameter per 10 ft of height** |

Some shafts run **vertical for the first 9–27 ft** and then break into batter. One 256 ft stone
chimney has no taper at all. Batter was checked by plumbing **every 3 ft** as it was built, which is
also why a stack that wandered is such a visible disgrace (see
[`16-how-it-was-actually-done.md`](16-how-it-was-actually-done.md)).

### Wall thickness steps, and the nine-inch top

A mill chimney is built as **three to seven discrete uniform-thickness sections**, each stepped
inward at a bed joint — not a smooth taper in section.

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| At the top, and 20 ft below it | **8½ in** — one brick |
| Then | **+4½ in per 20 ft downwards** |
| Fire-brick lining | **Additional to and independent of** the structure. Never counted |
| Cap | Also additional, and must not project further than the top wall thickness |

Attested ladders: 18 / 14 / 9 in on a 150 ft shaft; four 45 ft sections of 2 ft 3 in / 2 ft / 18 in /
14 in on a 180 ft one; **8 ft at the base to 1 ft 6 in at the top** on a 367 ft one; seven steps from
four bricks to one on a 120 ft one.

The top course is often just **9 in**, which is the ledge a steeplejack recounts lying across — legs
outside, head and shoulders in.

**Bond:** three or four stretcher courses to one header course. Hoop iron built in every few courses
is "to be commended", which is why so many surviving stacks are banded.

### The flue

The design rule is **6,000 cubic feet of hot gas per horsepower per hour**. Attested pairs:

| Height | Bore | Duty |
|---|---|---|
| 150 ft | 7 ft | 4 flue openings |
| 210 ft | 10 ft | 8 boilers at 40 hp |
| 300 ft | 9 ft | **15 boilers** |
| 69 m | 8 ft | 4 Lancashire boilers |

**The bore is usually parallel even where the outside batters** — the wall does all the thinning.
Flue openings at the base are about 7 ft high × 2 ft 6 in wide, 9 in firebrick sides, 14 in arch.

### Footings and plinth

Footings spread by regular offsets to a projection each side equal to the base wall thickness. One
worked 150 ft case: a 24 ft 4 in concrete raft, eight 11 in stones, brickwork starting at 21 ft 2 in
diameter and **stepping in nine offsets of 6 in each** to 16 ft 8 in. Plinths are typically
**parallel-sided with no batter**, 17–40 ft tall, moulded stone at the bottom and a moulded stone
cornice where the shaft springs.

## 2. The head — the silhouette everybody recognises

### The corbelled cap, fully dimensioned

From a 240 ft octagonal stone shaft:

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Projection per course | **~¾ in** |
| Height of the cap | **9 ft 9 in** (so roughly 29 courses at 4 in — *derived, not stated*) |
| Extreme projection | 15½ in |
| **Net projection beyond the receding face** | **18½ in** |
| Above the cap | the shaft continues **4 ft 6 in in 9 in work** |
| Decoration | **eight flat piers**, 11½ in wide, projecting a further 4½ in |

A cheaper cap, on a 250 ft circular brick shaft: a string course of **three salient brick courses 20
ft from the top**, then **four oversailing courses in the top 5 ft**, then a 3 in stone salient
course in clamped segments run with lead.

### Caps that are not brick

| Type | Dimensions |
|---|---|
| Stone cap | **7 ft deep, projecting 3 ft 6 in**; one was **16 ft deep** and its decay cost 41 ft of chimney |
| Cast iron | **2 tons** on a 108 ft shaft; **27 tons** on a 315 ft one |
| Copper rim | ⅜ in on a 2 ft 3 in blocking course |
| Fire-clay moulded | attested on an 1881 circular shaft |
| **Nothing at all** | one 1878 owner held "the plainer the termination the better" — a single firebrick course |

**Rules worth building to:** iron cramps are forbidden, because they oxidise and burst the stone —
copper or double-dovetailed slate dowels, two per joint. Every component's centre of gravity must
fall inside the outer circle of the shaft. The cap must deflect wind **upwards**. And the recurring
failure in the whole book: a heavy cap acts "as a weight at the end of a long lever", which is why so
many stacks were shortened.

### Decoration that is actually attested

Not generic Victoriana. These specific things: oversailings, string courses and dentils; three
salient brick courses as a string 20 ft down; flat pilaster piers on the cap; **bands of blue and
yellow brick on red, some in diaper pattern, over a rusticated rock-faced white stone base**; two
courses of white brick in a brick cap; iron bands up the shaft. Name and date stones go on the cap.

## 3. Colour

| Question | Finding |
|---|---|
| A working stack | Black to near-black. Soot "settled widely into the pores of the gritstone, creating black bitumen-like streaks" and turned whole districts "the colour of ash" |
| Where it is darkest | **The rain-sheltered surfaces.** Black crusts form "in sheltered areas, where moist air delivers a high concentration of dissolved pollutants" — so under the cap oversail and the string-course soffits |
| A top-to-bottom gradient | **Not found.** No source describes one. Treat any gradient as art direction, not as fact |
| Windward vs leeward | **Not found** as soot. The only attested weather-side difference is structural: "very frequent rains kept the mortar soft on the weather side… the joints on that side were squeezed rather closer" |

| Material | Colour |
|---|---|
| Accrington "NORI" | Dense deep red, smooth pressed face; fireclay, acid resistant — used for flue and chimney lining |
| Staffordshire blue | Deep blue from reducing-atmosphere firing; plinths, cappings, strings |
| Firebrick | **Colour not found.** 9 in lining, structurally independent, usually only the bottom 30–60 ft |
| Millstone grit, new | "Orange and gold, sparkling with silica"; Lancashire grits "grey to buff, locally with yellow or red overtones" |
| Grit, cleaned | Returns to "dark honey, mellow browns and tawny-greys" — **never the original colour** |
| Mortar | **Lime, not cement** — "cement is destroyed by a strong heat". 1:2 lime:sand, sometimes with smithy ashes, which measurably raised strength |
| Welsh slate | "Blue, purple or greenish-grey". Countess 20 × 10 in, Duchess 24 × 12, Princess 24 × 14 |

## 4. The setting

### Weaving sheds — the best-attested thing here, build straight to these

| | |
|---|---|
| Column grid | **3 m × 6 m** (later 6 × 6) |
| Ridge spacing | **3 m** |
| Clear height to gutter beam | **3.5 m** |
| Clear height to ridge | **4.6 m** |
| **Slated south slope** | **30°** |
| **Glazed north slope** | **65°**, span **1200 mm** |
| Columns | cast iron, **100 mm diameter**, hollow; the gutter beams double as rainwater gutters |
| Shed | 250 × 200 ft ≈ 1,200 looms |

Glazing faces **as near due north as possible**, for even light with no sun on the cloth. And the
detail that changes the silhouette completely: **the roofs sit behind blind stone parapets**, so from
the yard you often cannot see the saw-tooth at all. Floors are stone slabs on bare earth with no damp
course, and sheds were cut into slopes **on purpose**, because weaving wanted the damp.

### Spinning mills

| | |
|---|---|
| Bay, 1870 | **10 ft 6 in (3.20 m)** |
| Bay, 1907 | **22 × 15 ft 6 in (6.7 × 4.72 m)** — bays roughly double as steel replaces cast iron |
| Storey heights, 1907 | basement 8 ft 6 in, ground **16 ft**, spinning rooms 13 ft 9 in – 14 ft 3 in |
| Early steam mill | 25–68 m × 11.5–14 m, up to 8 storeys, floors 3.3 m falling to 2.75 m going up |

**Window width, pier width and cill height are not found anywhere.** What is attested: one window per
structural bay, rectangular with rectangular sills and lintels, **18-pane ground floor and 12-pane
above**. So window centres are bay centres — 3.2 m before about 1880, ~4.7 m after.

### The yard

| | |
|---|---|
| Lancashire boiler | **15–30 ft long × 5 ft 6 in – 8 ft 6 in diameter**; four of them at a 1907 spinning mill, two at a weaving mill |
| Boiler house | single storey, **wide arched openings, often facing the street so coal could be tipped from the road** |
| Engine house | a beam engine is tall and narrow; a horizontal engine is tall and **much broader**, long round-headed windows, glazed-tile dado, usually fronting the street with the name and date stone |
| Chimney position | linked to the boiler house by a flue that "could be of some length, and even underground" — so **the stack often stands detached and well away from the building**. A numeric distance is not found |
| The lodge | **200 × 82 ft**, rectangular, stone-rubble banked sides. It cools and recycles the engine's condensate and is sized to hold at least a day of it, with the hot discharge at the point **furthest from the inlet**. Depth not found |
| Entrances | **deliberately narrow** for horses, widened later for lorries |

### Terraces

| | |
|---|---|
| Carriage street (1875 byelaws) | **36 ft** minimum; back streets 16 ft and 13 ft |
| Front to front | 24 ft minimum, or ≥ the building height |
| House frontage | Manchester **12–15 ft**, Liverpool 10–12, Nottingham 11, Leeds ~15 |
| Ceiling heights | 8–9 ft ground and first, 7 ft to the second, cellar under 6 ft |
| **Chimney stacks** | **shared between handed pairs — two houses per stack** |
| Walls | 9 in single wythe; party wall 18 in above the roof; a ginnel every fourth house |
| Density | **70–80 houses to the acre**, max street length 120 yards |

**Not found, and not to be guessed:** terrace roof pitch in degrees, eaves and ridge heights, house
depth, stack height above the ridge, **pots per stack**, pot height, cill heights, door and window
sizes, and any numeric mill-to-terrace distance. A "14–16 ft frontage" figure circulates online and
traces only to an unreliable aggregator — **do not use it.**

## 5. Lattice and steel

**Gasholders.** The modelling ratio is standards against tank diameter, from attested pairs: 12/31 m,
13/32, 15/41, 16/44, 18/18, 22/66, 24/66, 28/62, 30/85. Horizontal girder tiers run from **two on a
small holder to five on the largest**. Standards are "just over 0.5 m at the base", tapering. Girders
are wrought-iron **60° triple-Warren lattice** — girder depth not found. Lift height ≈ tank depth.
Ornate frames carry full classical orders; one recorded set is **eighteen Tuscan columns about 36 ft
tall**. Spiral-guided holders (from 1890) have no frame at all: one 40.3 m holder stands **41.1 m
extended against 8.2 m collapsed**, with helical rails at **45°**.

**Water towers and yard gantries: not found.** No tank size, capacity or height to underside in any
source reached. The only lattice comparator is 1912 colliery headgear, about 30 m of riveted
wrought-iron lattice.

## One unresolved discrepancy, flagged rather than averaged

The famous 367 ft 6 in Bolton stack has **three different sets of dimensions** across three sources:
the 1885 treatise gives 112 ft girth at the base and 44 ft at the top; a later secondary account
gives 42 ft 6 in base and 24 ft top; a steeplejack's own account gives 127 ft round the base and 34
ft at the top. The treatise's figures came from the builders, so those are the ones to use — but if
that stack ever gets modelled, the discrepancy should be visible in the file rather than silently
resolved.
