# 18 — The trade on record

Research gathered 2026-09-20, following [`16-how-it-was-actually-done.md`](16-how-it-was-actually-done.md),
which covered one documentary about laddering a chimney. This one covers everything else: the jobs
that are not chimneys, the money, the day, and what actually killed people.

> **Names.** Rule 16. Every person in these sources is real and none is named. Firms are named only
> where a firm is the subject. What is recorded is the trade.

**How to read the source tiers.** *Primary* — trade standards read as documents (ATLAS ASG:001 on
ladders, ATLAS/CFA on anchors, ATLAS ASG:003 on steel chimney inspection, HSE HSG33), the 1881
Lightning Rod Conference Code, an 1885 treatise on tall chimney construction, an 1898 trade
pamphlet, a c.1885 first-hand memoir, and mill company minute books with actual tenders in them.
*Eyewitness* — forum accounts by people who were there, 1976–2011. *Indicative* — contractor
marketing and modern day rates, which are advertising.

---

## The thing that changes the design most

**Height carried almost no wage premium.** Men who climbed a 112 ft stack on driven staples and
repaired the coping, 1873, *"worked for 5/- a day, or little more than their ordinary wages"* — and
the account adds, pointedly, *"It was made a principle not to bribe anyone to undertake the job."*
An American survey of the trade in 1901 says the same: steeple-climbers *"taking it through the
year, get scarcely more than ordinary workmen's wages."*

The money was never in the danger. It was in the **contract price for the job**, and the job was
priced by the square yard for pointing and by the pound for ironwork — never, in anything found, by
the foot of height.

For a game with a career in it, that is the whole economy in one fact: **going higher does not pay
more.** Whatever makes a player climb the tall one has to be something other than the fee.

And the corollary, from a man who had seen a career of it: *"the only time he had seen anyone have
a serious fall was a bloke who dropped off the first ladder and fell about ten feet. Height had
nothing to do with danger."* Our nerve model scales drain with height. The trade says the risk
does not. Worth arguing about before the next tuning pass.

## Striking the stack — the stage the game does not have

[`17-the-long-game.md`](17-the-long-game.md) argues this is the missing act. Here is what the record
actually supports, because the first draft of that argument got it wrong.

**Timing.** Ladder a ~100 ft stack: **2 to 2.5 hours**, two men. **Strip it: about 30 minutes.** A
255 ft stack was laddered up one side in two hours by an expert in the 1890s.

**The procedure (ATLAS ASG:001).** The one genuinely load-bearing fact for design:

> Ladders come down in reverse order, **but the removal method is not necessarily the opposite of
> the installation method** — work done during the job may have created an obstruction that was not
> there on the way up. The method must be re-assessed, not assumed.

That is the trade telling us, in a safety standard, that the descent is its own problem. A game
that has just had you band a chimney has by construction changed the route down.

Also: **stand-offs work loose during removal** and are checked before the lashings come off; once a
ladder is disconnected from its anchors **nobody goes back on it**; sockets and hoops are never the
sole means of security even at the bottom; anchors come down **tied to the ladders or lowered in a
container**, never dropped.

**Gear does not always survive the strip.** Screw eyes *"should not be re-used as they have been
known to break on removal (but not during use)."* Nylon plugs are not reusable even if left in.
Self-tapping screws can go again once or twice — at **double the proof-test rate**.

**What gets left in the wall, and what that costs.** Dogs were frequently **just left in place** on
stacks a firm had a standing contract for. The recorded consequence is a photographed, badly
corroded dog on a Burnley mill stack, and the observation that *"old blacksmith made"* wrought-iron
dogs corrode far less than modern fabricated-and-welded ones. The next crew's consequence is
sharper: on a 1986 job at a Todmorden stack, *"They had used the old dog holes and it wandered a
bit. There were three rungs missing out of the top ladder as well."*

So the craft note in `16` — that keeping the stack straight is part of the job — has a second
cause. A wandering stack is not only a bad day's work; it can be **an inheritance from whoever
laddered it last.**

**Nightly partial striking, which is a mechanic on its own.** *"As soon as you ladder a chimney you
have to take precautions to stop access to the ladder. Most common was to remove the bottom ladder
each night or lash a scaffolding board to it blanking the rungs off. Otherwise some silly bugger
will attempt the climb."*

**Ladders left up between visits.** On regular-contract stacks the ladders stayed on the chimney
over winter. The first act of the new season was **checking every lashing, tightening them and
replacing some** — and men were told not to climb until that was done.

**What is not on record, and must not be invented:** drawing a dog, plugging or making good a hole,
any knot sequence for *undoing* a lashing, or any account of gear lost during a strike. The securing
lashing is specified in detail; nothing describes undoing it.

## The day, the week and the season

**Laddering rates.** 230 ft in about three hours; 255 ft in two hours, one side; a working jack
recalling *"i can remember laddering 10+ in a week."*

**Job durations, from named jobs:**

| Job | Duration |
|---|---|
| Weathervane off a 150 ft steeple and back | Thursday to Saturday |
| A 200 ft chimney job | a fortnight |
| Straightening a 132 ft octagonal stack, four men | three weeks, bad weather |
| Scaffolding 70 yards of a stack for belting | two to three weeks |
| Cutting the base and propping a 270 ft stack for felling | six men, eight working days |
| Hooping and pointing a 435 ft stack | nine months |
| Full refurbishment of a 367 ft stack | eight months |
| Taking a ~200 ft stack down by hand, single-handed | five months |

That distribution is **bimodal**, and it is exactly the shape the twelve-job campaign wants: a tail
of half-day-to-three-day vane, conductor and pointing jobs, and a small number of monsters that eat
most of a season.

**The season does not stop for winter — it stops for the customer.** This is the most useful and
least expected finding for the kettle. Chimney work is gated by the **mill's downtime**, not the
weather: the 1882 disaster that killed fifty-four happened on **28 December** precisely because the
repairs had been scheduled for the Christmas shutdown, when the mill was meant to be idle. It was
not. Men also worked *"top work at night, even during the winter, to save stopping the works during
the day time."* A winter lay-off is attested too, so the sources disagree — but the interesting
mechanic is unambiguous: **you work when they can afford to be cold.**

**And frost is sometimes an ally.** Removing a rotten brick extension at Todmorden, a jack *"waited
until there was a hard frost because it made it safer to ladder it, he said the ice was holding it
together. As he drove his dogs in he could hear dislodged bricks falling down the inside of the
shaft."*

**Wind stops the day, and the numbers are exact.** HSE HSG33, Beaufort as it applies to work at
height:

| Force | mph | m/s | The rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 13–18 | 5.5–7.9 | Special care with materials over 5 m long — *"approaching critical limit"* |
| 5 | 19–24 | 8–11 | *"approaching critical limit when all work must cease"* |
| 6 | 25–31 | 11.1–14.1 | *"No fixing work anywhere should be in progress"* |

HSE elsewhere: *"Winds in excess of 23mph (Force 5) will affect a persons balance."* ATLAS gives the
wind gradient as **10 m/s at 10 m → 11.4 m/s at 50 m → 12 m/s at 100 m**, and notes that **gusts can
be 60–100% greater** than the mean. ATLAS deliberately publishes no single stop-work number for
laddering, banding the Beaufort table green/yellow/red instead.

**Our own constants come out of this well.** `hud.gd` uses `WIND_CALM` 3.0 m/s and `WIND_STIFF` 12.0
m/s as the ends of the wind rose's colour ramp. 12 m/s is Force 6 — *"no fixing work anywhere"* — so
the dial reaches full red exactly where the trade stops work. That was arrived at by feel and it
lands on the standard. The gust swing the model now applies is also supported: gusts are both
stronger and from a different quarter.

The trade's own version, from a master to an apprentice: *"when the flaps of his leather jerkin
started flapping that was the time to get down."*

## The jobs that are not chimneys

Short procedural cores. [`05-mission-types.md`](05-mission-types.md) has the archetypes; this is
what the record says they actually involve.

### Lightning conductor — recommended as the next archetype to build

The 1881 Lightning Rod Conference Code gives a complete spec, and it is full of failure states:

- **Copper not less than 6 oz per foot run**, ≥90% conductivity. Iron alternative **2¼ lb per foot**.
- **Upper terminal blunt**, *"not sharper than a cone of which the height is equal to the radius of
  its base"*; a foot below it, a copper ring with three or four sharp points about 6 in long.
- **On a factory chimney specifically**: not one point but **a copper band round the top**, with
  stout points about 1 ft long **at intervals of two or three feet round the circumference**.
- **The curvature rule, which is a game mechanic as written**: *"In no case should the length of the
  rod between two points be more than half as long again as the straight line joining them."* No
  abrupt bends round sharp corners.
- **Holdfasts are metal, not insulators** — *"but the holdfasts should not be driven in so tightly
  as to pinch the rod, or prevent the contraction and expansion produced by changes of
  temperature."* Too tight is as wrong as too loose.
- **Clip spacing: 6 to 8 ft historically, 1000 mm in modern practice.**
- **The earth**: copper plate 3 ft × 3 ft × 1/16 in, in permanently wet ground, *"surrounded by
  cinders or coke"*, total copper surface not less than 18 sq ft. Same metal as the rod, always —
  *"otherwise destructive galvanic action sets in."*
- **The bottom 10 ft goes in an iron pipe**, against injury and theft.
- **Every joint soldered**, and best practice was **no joint at all** below the terminal.
- **Modern**: copper tape 25 × 3 mm (0.67 kg/m), at least two down conductors diametrically
  opposite, target **≤10 Ω overall**, test clamps at the earth pit opened with a 6 mm Allen key.

**Its failure modes are unusually good material.** A rod passing through a cornice in an undersized
hole rusted, expanded and burst the stone — *"the crack being covered up with soot this escaped my
observation"* — and a **48 lb** fragment fell and fractured the jack's knee. Flue acid ate a
terminal off the top of one stack, which was then struck four times with the rest of the rod
perfect. Earth resistance **rises in summer as the ground dries**, so a pass in March is a fail in
August. And a defective system is **worse than none**: it still attracts the strike and gives it no
path, producing side flash.

### Banding

Bands go on **starting at the top**, where the unrestrained edge is weakest. Post-tensioned **by the
bolts that join the segments** — *"loose bands are virtually worthless."* A refurbishment witnessed
on a 230 ft stack: bands off, cleaned, **two coats of bitumen**, back on using **two bosun's chairs
and laddering wedges to rest the band on while the bolts went in**, new bolts throughout.

**Octagonal stacks are the hard case**: *"a very difficult style to hoop, for one misfitting square
will throw the whole hoop out of form."*

**The failure is the bolt, and it is a set piece.** Fernbank Mill, 10 September 2011, filmed: the
topmost strap let go and crashed down where a man had been kneeling. *"From snapping to the strap
hitting the ground took six seconds"* — they timed it on the film. He heard the metal snap, looked
up and moved with a couple of seconds to spare. The diagnosis afterwards: *"the band bolt was the
problem, nothing left of it… the slightest tap would have snapped it, all down to lack of
maintenance for many years."*

No source gives a **band spacing rule**, and bands are demonstrably concentrated at the top. Do not
invent one. Heat-shrinking and turnbuckle tensioning are **not attested at all** — every retrievable
source describes bolted segmental bands.

### Repointing

Rake out **at least twice the height of the joint** (sources also say 25 mm or 2× width; one real
job cut back 20 mm over the top 20 m). **Hooked tools or chisels, never an angle grinder** — Historic
England is explicit that power tools do not clear the joint and damage the arris. Square-cut the
joint, brush out, dampen. Finish when the mortar *"can be marked with a thumbnail, but a thumb
pressed into the surface leaves barely any impression."* Then protect it: hessian, blankets or
carpet underlay, **misted regularly, minimum 48 hours** — one job wrapped the work in dampened
hessian **for three days**.

**And the failure is perfect for a weather clock:** *"Rain could wash fresh pointing work completely
off a stack before mortar cured."* A whole day's work, and the whole day's money, gone to a shower.

### Straightening a leaning stack

Cut a course out of the **convex** side, hold the gap on **graduated iron wedges** (one job used
wedges *"varying in size from three feet to six inches"*), withdraw the smallest first, refill with
a **thinner** course, and let gravity bring it back. A 120 ft stack 3 ft 1½ in out of plumb came
back *"not a single brick fractured."* Usually one cut; occasionally four. A 200 ft stack 4 ft 6 in
out took three. Cost about **one tenth of a rebuild** — £80 against £800.

The arithmetic was done in advance: on a 132 ft octagonal stack, *"it had been calculated that ¼"
would bring the stack back 7" at top"*, so the course substitution had to differ by 1⅝ in.

**It kills, and it kills late.** A stack cut in 1863 cracked three years later, cracked again in
1872, bulged in 1882, and on **28 December 1882** collapsed into a mill that was still working,
**killing 54 people**, average age 18, 41 of them women. The inquest returned accidental death and
added *"we regret that the works were not stopped during the repairs."* A separate 1873 job killed
the man doing it: the owner objected that the contractors were taking a whole course out and
wedging it rather than sawing; *"as if in ridicule of his assertion, the chimney toppled over."*

### Felling — and a doctrinal split worth keeping

The trade **disagreed with itself**, and both camps have documented failures. One jack never propped
and burned, cutting only — the *dry drop*. Others always propped and fired. An eyewitness to both:
*"The fellings I saw that had problems were always the ones that had been propped. Nothing worse
than the props burning out and the chimney staying up!"*

Prop-and-burn buys **predictable timing**, which is what makes it a show. Dry-cutting is *"a lot
safer than it sounds if done by skilled men as the stack moves very slowly at first"*, but *"you
can't forecast accurately when the stack will fall."*

**The tell-tale** is the instrument both methods share, and it is already half in our game as the
gob crack. A strip of wood or metal in the crack at the back, watched by one man with a **whistle**;
or fuse wire strung between two dogs twelve feet apart — *"when the wire broke it was time to
leave"*; or a thin timber from the top of the gob to the ground — *"when they heard the timber
crack, likewise it was time to go."* And the insight underneath it: *"the movement in the crack is,
in itself, weakening the structure all the time it is opening and closing."*

**Failure modes:** the **hang-up** (fire burns out, stack stands — one was saved twenty minutes
later by a gust of wind, and the cause was a **vitrified firebrick liner acting as reinforcement**);
**wind holding it up** from the gob side; **going early** (one fell on the first hammer blow after a
tea break); the **forward kick** as the top third hits; and the jack's nightmare, the **telescope**
— *"no warning, they just go."*

**Exclusion distance is genuinely unresolved.** Period sources say only *"a safe distance."* The
oft-quoted *"45° each side and 1.5× the height"* appears only in derivative training material and
**conflicts with HSE CIS45**, which states the zone *"is not simply related to the height"* of the
structure. Do not present 1.5× as the UK rule.

### Taking height off by hand

The record's best single piece of engineering, from 1885: bricks were to be salvaged on a crowded
site, so they built an **air-tight wooden chute down the flue** — 3½ × 5 in inside, planks 1½ in
thick, 12 ft lengths in cast iron sockets, caulked with tarred yarn, whole apparatus **about £6**.
A brick is 3 × 4½ in, leaving **¼ in each side for air to escape**, so *"the bricks being cushioned
in their fall, arrived at the base without damage."* A box at the bottom with a rubber-faced door;
the man below **rapped on the spout** when it was full. And the detail that makes it a stage:
*"The workman at top lowered his own scaffold, and as the spout became too high he cut a piece
off."*

The modern equivalent is blunter and just as usable: **drop the debris inside the flue and cap it
off when the flue is full.**

### Copings, caps and cramps

The job exists because **iron kills stone**. Cramps securing the coping *"corrode, and this
corrosion causes the stone to burst"*; the 1885 treatise had already said cramps should be copper or
slate dowel and *"on no account should iron cramps be used."*

The hazard is weight and sequence: *"on the top of a tall chimney the coping alone will weigh as
much as thirty tons"*, and the care is *"to ensure that the coping does not become top-heavy and
fall to the ground."* At Widnes part of a coping fell onto a scaffold; *"the workers were dashed to
the ground and killed on the spot."* In 1976 a third of a cast-iron chimney top let go with four men
standing on it — the jack **heard the bolts snapping** and got everyone off.

### Spires, vanes and gilding

You cannot circle a spire on ladders, but you can lay them **against** it — ladders go *"vertical to
industrial chimneys, toward the structure when installed to church spire"*, and angled back on a
cooling tower. Then the work goes over to a **bosun's chair**, *"so contrived that the man who is
using it has entire control of the ropes by which he is suspended."*

A vane comes down on **pulleys, rigged to keep it off the spire face the whole way**, and travels
**wrapped in foam in a purpose-built timber frame**. Gilding: **23–23.75 kt** for exterior work
(24 kt is too soft, though one cathedral's own press release says 24 — flagged), leaf about 0.0001
mm thick, 25 leaves to a book, oil size in 3-hour or 12-hour grades, laid when the size is *"slightly
tacky (similar to that of a post-it note) and not wet."*

The underlying defect is the same as everywhere else: the **ferrous centre rod rusts inside the
stone and splits it**, so the cure is to dismantle the top of the spire and put in stainless.

**And a gift for a survey mission:** the statutory five-yearly church inspection *"should be visual,
and such as can be made from ground level ladders or accessible roofs"*, and inaccessible parts
*"will not be opened up"* unless specifically requested. **The top of the spire is exactly what the
statutory inspection cannot see.** The defect is real, the schedule cannot find it, and it takes a
steeplejack to go and look.

## Anchors, in numbers

The best hard data found, from ATLAS/CFA guidance — a ready-made "can I fix here?" model:

| Rule | Figure |
|---|---|
| Drill position in a brick | on the centreline, **35–55 mm from the brick end** ("in the meat of the brick") |
| Minimum below the top of the structure | **280 mm** to an edge; **no less than 2 m** from the top of an unrestrained wall |
| Horizontal spacing | **350 mm** where joints are visible, **500 mm** rendered |
| Vertical spacing | **150 mm** visible, **190 mm** rendered |
| Max hole depth, rear brick of a 9 in wall | **180 mm** — *"any deeper risks breaking the back of the brick out"* |
| Never | two anchors in the same masonry unit |
| Proof test | **1.25–1.5× working load**, minimum 3 anchors and **1 in 20**; one failure doubles the rate |
| Drilling hazard | *"Powerful drilling machines used on rotary hammer in masonry with soft mortar joints may shake the masonry unit out of the mortar."* |

And from the ladder standard: **maximum 1.55 m vertical anchor interval**; **four anchors minimum
per ladder** on brick and stone (two upper, two lower); **all vertical load goes down the stiles to
the ground** — lashings take horizontal load only; **lashings minimum factor of safety 7×**, and
*"it is unlikely that 6 mm diameter rope lashings will give a satisfactory safety margin"*; **only
one person ever on the top ladder**; never ascend a ladder on a single temporary anchor.

That **1.55 m** is worth noting against the open question in [`BLOCKED.md`](../../BLOCKED.md) about
dog spacing. The modern standard and the 1970s documentary's *"five feet"* agree almost exactly. Our
2.5–6.0 m is the outlier, and it now has two independent sources against it rather than one.

## What was checked and is not there

Recorded so nobody spends the search again, and so nothing here gets invented later:

- **Drawing dogs, plugging holes, or undoing a lashing.** Not described anywhere.
- **Gear lost during a strike.** No account.
- **Band spacing up a stack.** No rule; bands cluster at the top.
- **Heat-shrunk or turnbuckle-tensioned bands.** Not attested. Bolted segments only.
- **Rope diameter for the Lancashire laddering system.**
- **Books of gold leaf consumed on a weathervane.** Searched twice.
- **Any historic weekly wage for a jack**, or any within-crew bonus or piecework structure.
- **Start times, meal breaks, or whether men came down for dinner.**
- **A formal boy/journeyman/master task ladder.** Indentures exist; a graded permission scheme does
  not appear.
- **Historic exclusion distance at a fire-felling.** Period sources say only "a safe distance".
- **Any documented failure of the wedge-substitution straightening method.**
- **Water towers.** No procedure, no pricing, no failure modes.

**Source cautions.** The 1898 pamphlet is promotional: its numbers are probably sound, its
superlatives are not — it claims its subject *"never had a real accident"* while recording his own
75 ft fall. A 1901 American survey describes a different tradition (stirrups and lassoing, not
ladders and dogs); do not blend the two. One widely-mirrored LLM-generated encyclopaedia is the sole
source for a "guy wires used to aim a felling" claim that **no period or practitioner source
supports** — treat it as fabricated. Wikipedia's articles on the trade contain no procedural
numbers at all.

**Live conflicts**, useful as design tension rather than errors to resolve: prop-and-burn versus dry
drop; ladder length (15 ft / 16 ft 6 in / 18–21 ft / 3–5 m / 10 ft, no canonical figure); lightning
system test interval (12 months / 2.5 years / 4 years, with ATLAS calling the standard
self-contradictory); chimney inspection interval (12 / 14 / 24 / 36 months by code); raking-out
depth; lean tolerance (25 mm or H/600 for steel, 1 mm in 100 mm for brick, 35 mm tolerated on a
historic half-brick shaft); gilding carat.
