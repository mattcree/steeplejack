# 19 — The complete game

Written 2026-09-21, answering the question that had not been asked plainly before: *what is the
whole of this game, what does finishing it mean, and what does somebody get out of having played
it?*

It is a plan, not a wish. Everything below is marked with what exists, because a content plan that
does not say what is already built is a way of feeling productive rather than being it.

---

## Part 1 — The loop, at three scales

A sim needs its loop to be legible at more than one zoom. Euro Truck has the mile, the delivery and
the ledger. This one has the minute, the job and the season.

### The minute — the verb loop

> **look → read → commit → hold on**

You point at a mortar joint. You *read* it: colour, the chalk of a previous tap, what the band is.
You **sound** it, and the note tells you what the brick is worth. You **commit** — drive a dog, and
the angle and force of every blow is yours. Then you **hold on** while you do it, because your grip
is draining the whole time and the wind is taking you off your line.

This is the part that works. A playtest called out the tapping and dogging as the thing that makes
it feel like a sim, and the instruction from that note is to keep going along those lines and not
to get clever.

**Built:** tap, hammer, lash, haul — and now strike, draw, clip, bolt and look. Nine verbs across
five archetypes, and the climb itself.

### The job — the four acts

From [`05-mission-types.md`](05-mission-types.md): **SET UP → THE FIDDLY BIT → THE COMPLICATION →
CLEAR UP.** [`17-the-long-game.md`](17-the-long-game.md) audited the game against it and found two
acts missing at either end.

| Act | What it is | State |
|---|---|---|
| **0. The yard** | Load the van. Twelve ladders or sixteen? | **Built** |
| **1. Laddering** | Dog, lash, haul, climb, sound | **Built.** The best thing in the game |
| **2. The work** | One of ten archetypes | **Five of ten** — survey, conductor, band, fell, straighten |
| **3. Striking** | Bring it all down and take it home | **Built** |

### The season — twelve jobs and a tarpaulin

Money and reputation carry. Nothing else does yet, and that is the gap
[`17`](17-the-long-game.md) Part 4 argues is the most valuable one to close: the district should
remember you, and the work should start coming to you rather than you going to find it.

#### J. STRAIGHTEN — "She's three foot out of plumb and they want her saved."

The real method, from period accounts: chisel **a full course of brick out of the convex side**,
hold the gap open on **graduated iron wedges** (one job used wedges from three feet down to six
inches), then **withdraw the smallest wedges first** and let gravity bring the stack back onto
itself. One cut usually; a 200 ft stack four foot six out of plumb took three. It cost about **a
tenth of a rebuild** — £80 against £800.

Why it belongs in this game and belongs *late*:

- **It is felling, inverted.** You cut into the base of a chimney with everything you learned
  killing chimneys — and this time if it comes down you have failed. Same geometry, opposite intent.
- **The arithmetic is the puzzle.** On one real job: *"it had been calculated that ¼ inch would
  bring the stack back 7 inches at top."* You choose the cut height and the course thickness, and
  the game tells you what that will do. Wrong by a little and it stands crooked the other way.
- **It has a real, slow, terrifying middle.** The stack settles over **18 to 36 hours**, oscillating
  — and at the highest cut on one job *"the oscillations frightened the masons, and they left the
  work. The slit became alternately wider and narrower by three-quarters of an inch."*
- **And it overshoots.** *"The chimney continued during several weeks to settle slightly in the
  direction opposite to its former inclination."* A perfect cut on the day can be wrong by the
  next.

It is also the one archetype where the client is saved money rather than sold a demolition, which
matters for the campaign's shape: see Part 4.

**Nine of twelve exist.** Three of those nine are in *italics*: the job is real and the level is
real, but it currently runs on an archetype that is built rather than the one it was designed for.
St Anne's wants the bosun's chair and ships as a spire conductor — which is the best-attested job
in the whole 1881 Code, so it is not a compromise so much as a different true thing. The Corn Mill
wants a mechanism and ships as a six-band job on an octagonal stack, which the trade calls the
awkward sort. Hollin Bank wants taking down by hand and ships as the survey that decides whether
it should be. Each becomes its designed archetype when that archetype exists; none of them is a
placeholder with nothing in it.

### And the arithmetic, which is the part that bites

The engine costs **£8,980**. The nine levels that exist pay **£8,994** in fees.

That is a margin of fourteen pounds, before a single pane of glass gets broken — so on the content
that ships today the campaign's payoff is *technically* reachable and *practically* is not, and it
only works at all because felling bonuses are on top of the fee. The economy was balanced for
twelve jobs and there are nine.

Reputation has the same shape: doing every job that exists earns **four stars**, and Great Aire's
gate is five. The board already handles this honestly rather than locking the player out — a gate
nothing in the level set can reach is reported as a gap, with the reason on the card, and you are
let through. But the five-star axis of completion in Part 3 cannot be finished until jobs 9, 10 and
11 land, and nor comfortably can the engine.

**Deliberate shape.** The three FELL jobs are 6, 7 and 12 — you learn to kill chimneys in the
middle of the campaign, then at 11 you are asked to save one, and only then does 12 ask you to take
down the biggest thing in the district. The order matters: STRAIGHTEN has to come *after* you are
fluent in felling or its inversion means nothing.

4. **Five archetypes** — GILD, TOP, MECHANISM, LATTICE, EMERGENCY. The actual content.

6. **Three more levels** — 9, 10 and 11, without which the engine and the fifth star are out of

## Part 3 — What 100% looks like

Five axes, and they are deliberately different in kind. Three can be completed by being thorough;
one by being good; one cannot be completed at all.

### 1. The work — twelve jobs done

The floor. Finish twelve jobs and the credits run. Most players will do this.

### 2. The craft — twelve jobs done *well*

Every job carries bonuses that are not money: the fell that lands inside the lines, the conductor
run that passes continuity first time, the vane that spins free and rings, the band that seats
without going oval, the straightening that comes back within an inch. **Twelve for twelve is the
completionist's target** and it is genuinely hard, because most of them are one-shot per job and
several are not retryable within a job.

### 3. The standing — five stars

Reputation, already built. Gains are first-time only, so five stars means **new work, not
grinding**. It is reached at about job ten if you have not put a chimney through a greenhouse.

### 4. The engine — forty parts, about £9,000

The traction engine under the tarpaulin, against ~£16,000 of career earnings. Finishable
comfortably on good work; impossible if you spend on kit and pay damages. **The credits sequence is
steaming it out of the yard and through the town, past every chimney in the game — most of which
are no longer there, because you took them down.**

### 5. The yard — one thing carried home from each job

Not bought. Earned by doing, and by *choosing* to bring it back. A finial off a spire. A weathercock
you re-gilded. A dog out of a stack that is not there any more. A length of the copper you ran. The
band bolt that nearly killed you. Twelve objects, and the yard is a museum of a trade that no
longer exists, assembled by the man who ended it.

### And the one that cannot be completed

**The notebook.** Every joint you sound, every stack you ladder, every method you learn goes in it.
It does not have a last page, because *knowing brickwork* does not have a last page, and a game
whose pillar is "the power fantasy is knowledge, not strength" should not hand out a certificate
saying you now know everything about mortar. It fills. It never fills up.

## Part 4 — Closure

Three questions, answered plainly, because "what does a person get out of playing this" is the one
that decides whether the other eighteen documents were worth writing.

### What the player *did*

They learned a real trade well enough to have opinions about it.

That is not a metaphor. By job twelve a player knows what a hollow note means, why a dog goes into a
wooden plug and not into mortar, why the lashing uses up all the rope (in a gale the loose end whips
and takes the whipping off), why the ladder is packed off the wall (so there is room for your boots
on the rungs), why you go plumb above the one below, and why a man drives the holes deeper the
higher he gets. *"I think it's called fear."*

None of that was on a skill tree. They cannot un-know it, and it is true outside the game, which is
the thing almost no game can offer.

### What the player *ended*

A district's worth of chimneys. That is the game's melancholy and it should not be softened.

Twelve jobs in, the skyline the player first drove into is mostly gone, and they are the reason. The
game never scolds them for this — the work was honest, the chimneys were dangerous, the money was
needed, and half of them were coming down whether or not this man did it. But it does not pretend
either. **The world shrinks as you get better at your job.** That is the whole of it.

This is why STRAIGHTEN has to be job eleven. One chimney, out of the whole campaign, that you were
called to *save* — and you save it with precisely the knowledge you built up killing the others.
It is the only job in the game where the skyline is the same at the end as at the start, and it
lands that way only because eleven jobs of practice have taught the player what that costs.

### What the player *keeps*

The engine, and the yard around it.

The engine has **zero mechanical benefit** and that is the point. It is the only thing in the game
that gets bigger. Every other number — chimneys standing, jobs left, men still in the trade — goes
down. A player who finishes it has spent a season's wages restoring a machine that does nothing, in
a yard full of pieces of buildings that no longer exist, and the last thing the game asks them to do
is drive it very slowly through a town they helped empty.

**The satisfaction is competence. The closure is that the competence outlived the thing that needed
it.** That is not a sad ending. It is the true one, and a game that earns it has said something
almost nothing else in the medium is set up to say.

## Part 5 — What to build, in order

Ordered by what unlocks the most, not by what is most fun to write.

| | Work | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The front end** — title, menu, options, continue | The game has no front door. Cheapest item here and the first thing anyone sees |
| 2 | **Striking a stack** | Gives every job a last act, and builds most of CONDUCTOR as a side effect |
| 3 | **The yard** — loadout, bench, board, kettle, tarpaulin | Unlocks the day clock, the ledger and the salvage. The whole meta layer is one scene |
| 4 | **CONDUCTOR** (job 2) | Proves the archetype pipeline with the cheapest new art in the set |
| 5 | **The persistent district** | Your dogs, your chalk, your bad line, still there next time |
| 6 | **BAND, TOP, GILD** (3, 4, 8) | The three that reuse the most existing verbs |
| 7 | **STRAIGHTEN** (11) | The keystone. Needs felling to exist first, and it does |
| 8 | **MECHANISM, LATTICE, EMERGENCY** (5, 9, 10) | The three with genuinely new systems |
| 9 | **The engine and the credits** | Last, because it is the payoff for all of the above |

**Nothing above is a decision this document gets to make on its own.** The twelve-job table, the
tenth archetype and the five axes of completion are proposals with reasons; the ones that change
